**Quick answer:** A 1099 offer is a business contract, not a job offer, so the rate is only one of the terms worth negotiating. Before you sign, pin down how you are paid and when, the volume you are guaranteed, who carries malpractice and the tail, what it costs to end the agreement, and any limit on where you can work next. ## Start with the comparison, then read the contract

If you are still choosing between contract and employee work, settle that first. The [1099 vs W-2 comparison](/resources/1099-vs-w2) prices self-employment tax, benefits and retirement on both sides, and the [1099 vs W-2 calculator](/tools/1099-vs-w2-calculator) runs your own figures. This guide starts once a 1099 offer is on the table.

## Classification: are you really a contractor?

The label on the contract does not decide your tax status. The IRS looks at the whole relationship, grouping the evidence into behavioral control, financial control and the relationship of the parties, and some states apply their own tests. An engagement that sets your schedule, supplies everything you use and directs how you see patients and chart can look like employment whatever the document calls it.

Read who controls your schedule and patient load, and ask whether other clinicians in the same role are employees. If the arrangement looks like employment, raise it before you sign: a misclassified contractor pays both halves of payroll tax on income an employer should have shared. The [IRS guidance on independent contractors](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee) sets out the factors.

## How you are paid

Contract NP work is priced in a few common ways, and each moves risk differently.

- **Hourly or per shift.** The most predictable. Confirm what counts as paid time: charting after the shift, inbox work, meetings and training.
- **Per encounter.** Rewards volume, and passes no-shows and slow days on to you. Ask for the scheduled volume in writing and what happens when the schedule is thin.
- **Share of collections.** Ties your pay to the practice's billing, which you do not control. Ask how collections are calculated, which payers count, how denials are handled, and whether you can see the reports behind your pay.

Whatever the structure, get the payment terms in writing: how you invoice, how long the practice has to pay, and what happens when a payment is late. A contractor who waits weeks to be paid is financing the practice.

## Guaranteed volume and cancellation

A high rate on hours that never materialize is worth less than a lower rate you can count on. Ask for a minimum number of shifts or hours per month, and for a clause that pays you when the practice cancels a scheduled shift on short notice. Read the other direction too: how much notice you must give to drop a shift, and whether the agreement penalizes it.

## Malpractice and the tail

Ask which policy covers you, whose name is on it, and whether it is occurrence or claims-made. A claims-made policy covers claims only while it is in force, so ending it without tail coverage (an extended reporting endorsement) leaves your past patients uncovered. Put in writing who buys the tail when the agreement ends, and check that the limits meet what your state and your payers require. Our [malpractice insurance guide](/blog/np-malpractice-insurance-guide) explains both policy types.

## Collaboration and supervision costs

Where your state requires a collaborative agreement or supervision, somebody has to arrange it and pay for it. Ask whether the practice provides the collaborating or supervising physician, whether any fee is charged back to you, and what happens to your engagement if that relationship ends. The rule differs by state, even within one practice authority tier, so check your state's entry in the [scope of practice explorer](/scope-of-practice).

## Indemnification, insurance and records

Contractor agreements often ask you to indemnify the practice, meaning you cover its losses from your work. Narrow that clause to your own negligence, and make it mutual where you can. Check any requirement to carry your own general liability or business insurance, who owns and keeps the medical records, and whether you can access them if a claim or board complaint arrives after the engagement ends.

## Termination and restrictive covenants

Read how either side can end the agreement, with notice, for cause or both, and how much notice each side owes. A short notice period for the practice and a long one for you is a term worth trading. Non-compete and non-solicitation clauses deserve particular care, because their enforceability is set by state law and differs widely. A limit on the radius or the employers you can work for after the contract ends can cost more than the rate you negotiated, so have any restrictive covenant reviewed by an attorney who works with clinicians before you sign.

## Credentialing and onboarding time

Payer credentialing and facility privileges take time before you can bill for a single visit. Ask who pays for that time, who pays application and licensure costs, and whether any of it is clawed back if you leave early.

## Negotiate the package, then put it in writing

Negotiate the rate and the terms in the same conversation, and trade one against the other: a lower rate for a guaranteed minimum, or a higher rate if you carry your own malpractice. ## Frequently asked questions

### What should a 1099 nurse practitioner contract include?

The rate and how it is calculated, invoicing and payment terms, guaranteed volume and cancellation terms, the malpractice policy and who buys the tail, any collaboration or supervision arrangement and its cost, indemnification, records access, termination notice, and any restrictive covenant.

### Can I negotiate a 1099 NP contract?

Yes. A contractor agreement is a business contract between two parties, and most of its terms can be negotiated. Guaranteed volume, cancellation pay, payment timing, tail coverage and notice periods are all fair to raise alongside the rate.

### Who pays for malpractice tail coverage on a 1099 contract?

Whoever the contract says. If the policy is claims-made, agree in writing who buys the tail when the engagement ends. If the contract is silent, plan on buying it yourself.

### Is a non-compete in a 1099 NP contract enforceable?

It depends on the state and on the clause. Enforceability of non-compete and non-solicitation clauses is set by state law, and courts look at the scope, duration and radius. Have an attorney review any restrictive covenant before you sign.

### How do I know whether a 1099 role pays more than a W-2 job?

Price both packages the same way: self-employment tax, malpractice, health insurance, retirement and unpaid time off on the contractor side, against salary and benefits on the employee side.
