**Quick answer:** Apply the accreditation filter first, because it sits upstream of everything else — certification eligibility, licensure, federal student aid, and whether your credits ever transfer. Verify it in the accreditor's own directory with a date attached, never on the program's marketing page. Then ask one hard question about clinical placement, and check how the program's geography lines up with where you actually intend to practice. This page names no schools and ranks no programs. It is the procedure, so you can run it yourself on whatever list you are looking at.

---

## Three different things get called "accredited"

| Layer | Who grants it | What it governs |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional accreditation | An accreditor recognized for the institution as a whole | Federal student aid eligibility, and whether credits are likely to transfer |
| Programmatic nursing accreditation | A nursing accreditor, for that specific program | Whether the program meets the profession's education standards — the layer certification eligibility turns on |
| Board approval | Your board of nursing | Whether that program is accepted for licensure purposes in that jurisdiction |

A school can hold one and not another. When a page says "fully accredited" without naming the accreditor and the layer, it has told you nothing — treat that sentence as a red flag rather than reassurance.

## Who the nursing accreditors are

- **CCNE.** The [Commission on Collegiate Nursing Education](https://www.aacnnursing.org/ccne-accreditation), an autonomous accrediting arm affiliated with AACN, accredits baccalaureate, graduate, and post-graduate APRN certificate programs in nursing.
- **ACEN.** The [Accreditation Commission for Education in Nursing](https://www.acenursing.org/) accredits nursing programs across every level of nursing education.
- **NLN CNEA.** The [Commission for Nursing Education Accreditation](https://cnea.nln.org/) also accredits nursing programs at every level. Searching only the first two directories is how a legitimately accredited program gets mistaken for an unaccredited one.
- **Track-specific accreditors.** Nurse anesthesia programs are accredited by the [COA](https://www.coacrna.org/), and midwifery programs by ACME. If you are heading toward CRNA or nurse-midwifery, that is the directory that governs your path.
- **Confirming the accreditor itself.** An agency's recognition status is public: search the U.S. Department of Education's [database of accredited postsecondary institutions and programs](https://ope.ed.gov/dapip/) rather than taking any agency's word for its own standing.

## Status is what you check, not the label

Accreditation is a status with a date on it, not a permanent property of a school. Agencies grant it initially, continue it after review, place programs under warning or probation, and withdraw it. Each accreditor publishes its own vocabulary and its own current list.

- Look the program up in the accreditor's directory yourself, note the exact degree level listed, and save the page with the date you checked. A program's own website is marketing, not evidence.
- Confirm the specific track, campus, and delivery mode you are applying to. An institution can hold accreditation for one program while another is not covered.
- Treat "accreditation pending," "seeking accreditation," and "candidate" as open questions, not near-certainties. Ask the program, in writing, what happens to your cohort if the decision goes against them.
- Re-check before you accept an offer, and again before you register. Status can move while you are deciding.

## What accreditation decides downstream

- **Certification eligibility.** The body that will hold your exam application publishes the education rule and enforces it — the [AANP Certification Board](https://www.aanpcert.org/) and [ANCC](https://www.nursingworld.org/ancc/) certify nurse practitioners, [NBCRNA](https://www.nbcrna.com/) certifies CRNAs, and [AMCB](https://www.amcbmidwife.org/) certifies certified nurse-midwives. Confirm the current rule with that body before you enroll. A wrong accreditation assumption is costly because it surfaces at the moment you are least able to fix it.
- **Licensure.** Your board of nursing decides whether it will license a graduate of a given program, and board approval is a separate determination from accreditation. Reach yours through the [NCSBN](https://www.ncsbn.org/) directory or the [licensure checker](/tools/licensure-checker).
- **Federal student aid.** Aid eligibility runs through the institution's accreditation rather than the nursing program's. Check the school itself on [studentaid.gov](https://studentaid.gov/).
- **Transfer and doctoral admission.** Credits earned at an institution whose accreditation a receiving school does not accept may not move with you.

## Clinical placement is the second filter

Placement support separates programs that look alike on paper. Ask, and get answers in writing:

- Does the program secure clinical placements, or am I responsible for finding my own?
- If the program places students, in which geography — and what happens if it cannot place me?
- If I must find my own, what support exists, and does the school already hold affiliation agreements near me?
- What is the policy on paid third-party placement services?
- What happens to my progression and my tuition if a placement falls through mid-term?
- How are preceptors approved, and how long does approval take in practice?

The mechanics behind every one of those answers — the affiliation agreement, preceptor approval, and the four parties involved — are in the [preceptor and clinical placement guide](/blog/np-preceptor-guide). Read it before an admissions call and you will get specific commitments instead of reassurance.

## Where you study interacts with where you will practice

The [AANP](https://www.aanp.org/advocacy/state/state-practice-environment) classifies 27 states + DC as full practice authority (AANP State Practice Environment, 2025), and some full-practice states phase authority in through a transition-to-practice period, so day-one independence is not uniform inside that count — the [full practice authority guide](/resources/fpa-guide) has the per-state picture. That landscape shapes what a new graduate's first role looks like far more than a program's ranking does.

- **Clinical hours completed in one jurisdiction do not automatically satisfy another's licensure route.** The receiving board decides, so if you intend to move, ask that board before you choose the program.
- **Distance programs still place students somewhere physical.** Confirm the program can support placement in the state you actually live in.
- **Look at the market you are aiming at, not the national one.** Compare tracks with the [specialty pay comparison](/salary-guide/specialty), read the [track comparison guide](/blog/fnp-vs-pmhnp-vs-agacnp), and see what employers are actually hiring for on the [new-grad board](/jobs/new-grad).

## Verify these yourself before you enroll

- The program's listing in its accreditor's directory, at your degree level and track, saved with the date.
- The accreditor's own recognition status in the Department of Education database.
- Your certifying body's current education requirement for the credential you want.
- Your board of nursing's position on graduates of that program.
- The placement policy, in writing, including what happens when placement fails.
- Total cost of attendance from the financial aid office, in writing, rather than a per-credit figure from a brochure.

Once you are licensed, the same verify-at-the-source habit carries into your first job: the [credentialing checklist](/blog/np-credentialing-checklist) covers the document set employers and payers verify, and much of it is the paperwork your program generated.

If you work on the other side of this — a program director or career-services team placing graduates — the employer-side view of the same market, plus a free jobs widget for your career-services page, is on the [page for programs](/for-programs).

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I check whether an NP program is accredited?

Search the accreditor's own directory rather than trusting the program's website. Nursing programs are accredited by CCNE, ACEN, or NLN CNEA; nurse anesthesia programs by the COA; midwifery programs by ACME. Confirm the exact degree level and track you are applying to, note the status and the date you checked, and separately confirm the institution's accreditation if federal aid or credit transfer matters to you.

### Does it matter which nursing accreditor a program holds?

What matters is that the program holds current programmatic accreditation from a recognized nursing accreditor, and that your certifying body and your board of nursing accept it. Because those two organizations set the rule you will be held to, confirm eligibility with them directly rather than assuming any one accreditor is universally preferred.

### What does "accreditation pending" mean for an applicant?

It means a decision has not been made, and the applicant carries the risk. Ask the program in writing what happens to enrolled students if accreditation is not granted, whether a teach-out arrangement exists, and how graduates of the pending cohort would establish certification eligibility. Then take those answers to the certifying body before you accept a seat.

### Should I choose a program that arranges my clinical placements?

For most applicants, guaranteed placement is worth real money, because an unplaced student cannot progress no matter how good the coursework is. But confirm what the guarantee covers: the geography, what happens when it fails, and whether tuition continues during a gap. A program that is candid about its limits is often a better bet than one that promises broadly.

### Does the state where I study decide where I can practice?

No — licensure is granted by the board of nursing in the state where you intend to practice, and each board sets its own route. Studying elsewhere is normal, but confirm with the receiving board that its route accommodates your program and your clinical hours before you enroll, especially if you plan to move immediately after graduation.
