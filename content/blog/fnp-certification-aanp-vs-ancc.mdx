**Quick answer:** "AANP vs ANCC" means AANPCB vs ANCC. AANPCB (FNP-C), now operating as NPCB, gives the shorter exam: all multiple choice, built on four clinical practice domains. ANCC (FNP-BC) gives the longer exam, and its outline spells out more legal, ethical and professional practice content. Both certify the same population focus; confirm which your board of nursing accepts.

---

## What "AANP vs ANCC" actually compares

The American Association of Nurse Practitioners (AANP) is a membership association, and it certifies nobody. The exam people call "the AANP" belongs to the [American Academy of Nurse Practitioners Certification Board](https://www.aanpcert.org/) (AANPCB), an independent board affiliated with AANP that does business as the Nurse Practitioners Certification Board (NPCB). The other family nurse practitioner board is the [American Nurses Credentialing Center](https://www.nursingworld.org/ancc/) (ANCC), affiliated with the American Nurses Association. Either credential, FNP-C from AANPCB or FNP-BC from ANCC, makes you a nationally certified FNP; the [NP certification overview](/blog/np-certification-overview) covers the other tracks.

## AANPCB vs ANCC at a glance

| What to compare | AANPCB (FNP-C) | ANCC (FNP-BC) |
|---|---|---|
| Exam length | Shorter: fewer questions, less testing time | Longer: more questions, more testing time |
| Question format | Multiple choice throughout | Computer-based; the outline lists domains, not item formats |
| Content | Assess, diagnose, plan and evaluate, weighted by patient age | Assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation and evaluation |
| Professional practice | Legal and ethical issues among its applied knowledge areas | Legal, ethical and regulatory knowledge, APRN scope and standards, health IT |
| Renewal | NP practice hours plus continuing education with pharmacology, or retesting | Continuing education with pharmacology, plus one professional development category |
| Member discount | AANP and AAENP members | ANA, AANP, AANP student and GAPNA members |

## How the two FNP exams differ

AANPCB's FNP exam is the shorter of the two, and every question is multiple choice; ANCC's has more questions and more testing time. Both include unscored pretest questions, and ANCC notes that you cannot tell them apart from scored ones, so answer every question. The exact counts, times and fees are in the [NPCB FNP Candidate Handbook](https://www.aanpcert.org/wp-content/uploads/FNP-Candidate-Handbook-06-04-2026.pdf), on the [NPCB certification page](https://www.aanpcert.org/certify/) and on [ANCC's FNP certification page](https://www.nursingworld.org/our-certifications/family-nurse-practitioner/); check them before you schedule.

The content is organized differently. AANPCB builds its FNP blueprint on four practice domains (assess, diagnose, plan and evaluate) and spreads the scored questions across patient ages from newborn to older adult, with the most weight on adults; legal and ethical issues appear among its applied knowledge areas. ANCC's outline, effective October 30, 2026, uses five domains ([ANCC FNP Test Content Outline](https://www.nursingworld.org/globalassets/study-aids/family-nurse-practitioner-tco_04062026.pdf)). Implementation, the largest, pairs treatment and patient education with legal and ethical implications, regulatory guidelines such as abuse reporting, APRN scope and standards, and the responsible use of health IT, including artificial intelligence. Both exams test clinical care; ANCC's outline names more professional practice content.

Neither board reports your result as a percentage of correct answers. AANPCB sets its passing score with a recognized standard-setting method and gives a preliminary pass or fail right after the exam, official once NPCB reviews your file. ANCC's exams are criterion-referenced, so you are measured against a fixed standard rather than other candidates, and a failing report includes feedback by content area ([ANCC Certification Handbook](https://www.nursingworld.org/globalassets/certification/ancc-certification-handbook.pdf)).

## Which FNP exam is easier?

No board publishes a difficulty ranking, and each exam is scored against its own standard, so "easier" comes down to fit. AANPCB's blueprint may suit you if your strength is clinical decision making across the lifespan and you prefer a shorter sitting; ANCC's may suit you if your program also covered professional role, regulation and ethics in depth. Both boards publish first-time results by exam: read [AANPCB's 2025 statistics](https://www.aanpcert.org/wp-content/uploads/AANPCB-2025-Certification-Statistics.pdf) and [ANCC's 2025 certification data](https://www.nursingworld.org/globalassets/docs/ancc/ancc-cert-data-website.pdf) at the source, not on a forum.

## Eligibility: what both boards require

- **An accredited graduate FNP program.** ANCC accepts an FNP master's, post-graduate certificate or DNP accredited by CCNE, ACEN or the NLN CNEA; for a certificate, only the certificate program's own accreditation counts. AANPCB accepts a graduate, postgraduate or doctoral FNP program from an accreditor recognized by the U.S. Department of Education or the Council for Higher Education Accreditation. [Verify accreditation before you choose a program](/blog/how-to-evaluate-np-programs).
- **The APRN core.** Graduate courses in advanced pathophysiology, advanced health assessment and advanced pharmacology.
- **Faculty-supervised clinical hours.** Each board's handbook sets the minimum.
- **An active RN license.** Both boards require one.
- **Timing.** AANPCB lets you apply before you finish and test once all coursework and clinical hours are complete, even before your degree is conferred.

## How renewal works

Each credential runs for a fixed certification period set in the board's handbook, and both can be renewed without retesting.

- **AANPCB.** Practice hours in your NP role plus continuing education that includes pharmacology, or retake the exam ([NPCB FAQ](https://www.aanpcert.org/resources/faq/)).
- **ANCC.** Mandatory continuing education, part of it in pharmacology for NPs, plus one professional development category, such as academic credits, preceptor hours, practice hours or retesting; practice hours are an option, not a requirement ([ANCC Certification Renewal Handbook](https://www.nursingworld.org/globalassets/certification/renewals/ancc-certrenewalrequirements.pdf), effective September 10, 2025). From February 1, 2027 the options regroup into three pathways: additional continuing education, practice or scholarship ([ANCC](https://www.nursingworld.org/globalassets/certification/renewals/ancc-certification-renewal-handbook_2027.pdf)).
- **Your license renews separately.** The [state CE hub](/blog/np-ceu-requirements-by-state) links every board's rules, and the [credentialing checklist](/blog/np-credentialing-checklist) covers what verifiers check.

## Do employers and boards of nursing accept both?

AANPCB's handbook says its certification programs are accredited by the Accreditation Board for Specialty Nursing Certification (ABSNC) and the National Commission for Certifying Agencies (NCCA), and ANCC says its APRN certifications hold both accreditations and are accepted by NCSBN and state boards of nursing. Acceptance is still your board of nursing's decision, so confirm it with the [licensure checker](/tools/licensure-checker) and the board's own site; [NCSBN](https://www.ncsbn.org/) maintains the directory of boards. If a job posting names one board, ask the employer's credentialing office whether the other is accepted.

## How to choose

1. **Start with your board of nursing.** Check its accepted list with the [licensure checker](/tools/licensure-checker) and on the board's own site.
2. **Ask your program.** Faculty know what recent graduates sat for and why.
3. **Read both outlines and try the practice questions.** AANPCB offers optional practice exams and ANCC publishes sample questions.
4. **Ask a target employer, if you have one.** Credentialing offices answer this definitively and at no cost.
5. **Then stop optimizing.** The [family practice job market](/jobs/family-practice) hires both credentials, and the [FNP salary page](/salary-guide/specialty/family-practice) shows what the roles pay. Your population focus (see [FNP vs PMHNP vs AGACNP](/blog/fnp-vs-pmhnp-vs-agacnp)) shapes your career far more than the letters after it, and [how to become a nurse practitioner](/blog/how-to-become-a-nurse-practitioner) covers the steps around the exam.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the AANPCB or ANCC FNP exam easier?

No board publishes a difficulty ranking, and each exam is scored against its own standard. AANPCB's exam is shorter and entirely multiple choice; ANCC's is longer, and its outline names more professional practice content. Choose the outline that matches how you trained, and read each board's published first-time results at the source.

### What is the difference between FNP-C and FNP-BC?

FNP-C is the family credential from AANPCB, whose handbook names the registered mark NP-C, and FNP-BC is the board certified credential from ANCC. Both certify the same population focus and make you a nationally certified FNP; the differences are in the exams and renewal rules, not in what you may do in practice.

### Is FNP-C or FNP-BC better?

Neither, in any way an authority publishes. Both are national certifications for the same population focus, and scope of practice comes from state law and your license. The useful questions are narrower: does your board of nursing accept it, and does the exam's content outline match how you trained?

### Can I hold both FNP-C and FNP-BC?

Yes. They are independent credentials from independent boards, each with its own application, fees, exam and renewal rules, so holding both doubles the maintenance. Unless an employer asks for both, one is enough to be nationally certified.

### What happens if I let my FNP certification lapse?

AANPCB says an expired certificant may not use the NP-C credential and must meet current eligibility rules to regain certification, and ANCC's reactivation policy may require passing the exam again. The bigger risk is your license, because boards generally tie APRN licensure to current national certification, so contact your board of nursing first.
