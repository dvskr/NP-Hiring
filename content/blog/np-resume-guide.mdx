**Quick answer:** Your NP resume is read twice before anyone forms an opinion — once by a parser that turns it into database fields, once by a reviewer who skims it before deciding whether to read it properly. Both reward the same thing: a single-column layout, standard section headings, Month YYYY dates, and a credentials block that spells out license type, state, certification, and certifying body. Put credentials above experience; everything else is detail.

---

## Two readers, one format

Where an employer recruits through an applicant tracking system — Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, Workday and the rest — your resume is parsed before a person sees it. The parser's job is to extract structured fields — name, contact, degrees, employers, dates, licenses, certifications — and anything it can't parse either lands in the wrong field or vanishes. When you upload a resume to your profile on this board, the same thing happens: the parser reads your name and contact details, your NPI and DEA if present, every state license, your certifications, education, and work history, then shows you a review screen before anything saves.

That is a useful test. If the extraction comes back with your licenses jumbled, a hospital's ATS will do worse. Fix the resume, re-upload, and check again.

The human reader wants something different: evidence you can carry the panel they're hiring for. The good news is that these two audiences do not conflict. Clean structure serves both.

## The structure that parses cleanly

Use this order. It puts the qualifying facts where both readers look first.

1. **Name and credentials line** — "[First Last], MSN, APRN, FNP-BC". Post-nominals belong here, not only in a certifications section.
2. **Contact** — phone, professional email, city and state, LinkedIn URL. One line, no icons.
3. **Professional summary** — three or four lines. Setting, population, years, and what you're targeting.
4. **Licenses and certifications** — the credentials block, described below.
5. **Clinical experience** — reverse chronological, NP roles first, RN roles after.
6. **Education** — degree, school, graduation month and year.
7. **Optional sections** — clinical rotations (essential for new grads), procedures and skills, EMR systems, publications, precepting, professional memberships.

Formatting rules that prevent silent data loss:

- **One column.** Two-column layouts interleave text when parsed, so your employer names end up inside your skills list.
- **No tables, text boxes, or graphics.** Content inside them is frequently invisible to a parser. Skill-rating bars carry no information anyway.
- **Nothing critical in headers or footers.** Many parsers drop that region entirely — including the page with your phone number in it.
- **Standard headings.** "Clinical Experience" parses; "Where I've Made an Impact" does not.
- **Month YYYY dates**, consistently, with an en dash between them. "March 2021 – Present".
- **Standard degree abbreviations** — BSN, MSN, DNP, PhD, ADN — because that's what the field expects to match.
- **PDF unless told otherwise.** For this board, PDF or Word (.pdf, .doc, .docx) up to 5 MB. If an application form specifies a format, follow it.

## The credentials block

This is the section that most NP resumes get wrong, and it's the one credentialing staff read first. List each license and certification on its own line, with the elements each reader needs:

- **License:** type, state, and expiration month and year. "APRN, Texas — exp. 06/2027".
- **RN license:** same treatment, including multistate status if your primary state of residence participates in the Nurse Licensure Compact.
- **Certification:** the credential and the certifying body. NPs certify through AANP or ANCC; nurse anesthetists through NBCRNA; certified nurse-midwives through AMCB. Name the population focus — it is a distinct credential and employers credential to it.
- **DEA:** state "Active" or the state it's held in.

> **Do not put license, NPI, or DEA numbers on a resume you email or upload to job boards.** They are identifiers that belong in a credentialing packet you send to a specific verified employer, not in a document that gets forwarded. Employers who need them ask. The same rule covers anything you paste into an AI drafting tool.

## Bullets that survive the skim

The difference between a resume that reads as capable and one that reads as generic is quantification. Reviewers are calibrating your throughput and acuity.

Weak: *Provided care to a diverse patient population in a fast-paced clinic.*

Strong: *Managed a panel of ~1,400 adults in an FQHC, averaging 18 visits/day with same-day acute slots; led diabetes group visits that the site continued after my transition.*

Anchor every role in four things: **volume** (panel size, visits per day, census), **acuity and population**, **the systems you worked in** (EMR, referral workflows), and **an outcome or ownership signal** (a protocol, a program, a quality metric, precepting). Lead each bullet with a verb, keep it to two lines, and cap most roles at four to six bullets.

If you're a new grad NP, your rotations are your clinical experience. Give each one a site type, a population, hours, and what you were trusted to do independently — then keep your RN history immediately below it, because bedside years are the differentiator between you and other new grads. ## Tailoring without rewriting

Keep one master resume and change three things per application: the summary's target line, the order of bullets inside your most recent role, and the terminology. If the posting says "primary care," don't say "ambulatory" — parsers and reviewers both match on the employer's words. Mirror their language where it's honestly true of you, and never where it isn't.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should an NP resume be?

Two pages is normal and expected for a clinician with a license block, education, and RN history. New grads usually land on one to two pages; NPs with a decade of experience, precepting, and publications can justify three. Length is never the problem — unquantified bullets are.

### Should I put my license number on my resume?

No. List the license type, state, and expiration date, but keep license, NPI, and DEA numbers out of any document you upload to a job board or email to a recruiter. Provide them during credentialing, to a verified employer.

### PDF or Word for an NP resume?

PDF unless the employer specifies otherwise — it preserves your layout across systems. Modern parsers handle text-based PDFs well. What breaks parsing is a scanned or image-based PDF, so make sure your text is selectable before you send it.

### How do I write an NP resume with no NP experience?

Move clinical rotations above work experience and treat them as clinical experience: site type, population, hours, and what you managed independently. Keep your RN roles directly below with acuity and ratios. That combination is what new-grad-friendly employers are actually evaluating.

### Should I include an objective statement?

Use a professional summary instead. An objective describes what you want; a summary describes what you bring and where you're aiming, which is more useful to both the parser and the reviewer in the same three lines.

### How do I list multiple state licenses?

One line per license, each with the state and expiration date, most relevant to the role first. If your RN license is multistate under the Nurse Licensure Compact, say so — it shortens the employer's mental math on your start date.
